                                                Abstract

     Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is often described as a system improving the process by which
 it improves itself. That description identifies a feedback loop but leaves unresolved which changes
 count as improvement, who may alter the criteria, how authorization persists across self-modification,
 and when recursion becomes criterion drift. This paper supplies a formal, auditable answer: Recursive
 self-improvement is lawful continuation improving the machinery of lawful continuation. “Lawful” denotes
 root-anchored authorization and satisfaction of governing binding conditions within a declared system
 boundary. Improvement is separately certified by a protected verifier against a protected evaluation
 vector. The framework specifies a finite protected trust structure R → Ht → (Ct , Et , Vt ) governing
 candidate transitions Ut : Mt → Mt+1 , where the root boundary R anchors higher-order authorization
 Ht , binding conditions Ct , a protected evaluation vector Et , and verifier Vt . Improvement is vector-
 valued: at least one designated dimension must improve, every protected dimension must remain
 within an explicit tolerance, and mandatory invariants must remain satisfied. A transition constitutes
 self-improvement only when the current continuation machinery materially participates in producing
 its accepted candidate update. Recursion requires the resulting improved machinery to materially
 participate in producing a subsequent accepted update under the governing conditions. Five principles,
 a binary decision procedure, adversarial cases, and a prospective test protocol distinguish lawful
 RSI from ordinary learning, one-shot self-modification, AI-assisted engineering, recursive capability
 amplification, reward tampering, evaluator capture, and recursive drift. The paper defines RSI, makes
 claimed instances empirically testable, and states explicit conditions under which the classification
 framework would require revision.

1    Introduction

An improving system can become more capable while becoming less controllable. It can raise a benchmark
score by exploiting the benchmark, preserve a numerical reward by corrupting the reward channel, or
certify its own update after replacing the certifier. Each can create an apparent improvement signal;
when embedded in a feedback loop, that error can compound.
The central problem is therefore not recursion alone. It is continuity of authority, conditions, evaluation,
and verification while the machinery producing future continuations changes. The proposed definition
is:

    Canonical RSI definition

       Recursive self-improvement is lawful continuation improving the
                       machinery of lawful continuation.

Terminological scope. This paper proposes a technical definition of RSI; it does not assert that prior
literature already uses the term this narrowly. Broader usage may include self-directed recursive capability
growth that is unsafe, criterion-drifting, or self-certifying. Within the present framework, those processes
are classified separately as recursive capability amplification or recursive drift.
Under the binding-condition framework, RSI denotes the lawful recursive class defined here; compound-
ing self-directed capability change that fails the authorization, verification, preservation, or recursion
requirements is classified separately as recursive capability amplification or recursive drift.
This definition separates two questions that are frequently collapsed:
1. Did the system contribute to a change in its own continuation machinery?
2. Did that change remain authorized, verifiably beneficial, and condition-preserving under a pro-
   tected regime?
The first question concerns self-modification. The second asks whether that transition constitutes lawful
self-improvement. RSI additionally requires the improved machinery to materially participate in produc-
ing a subsequent accepted improvement. The distinction matters because objective corruption, reward
tampering, evaluator replacement, and benchmark gaming can all generate apparent improvement while
destroying the basis on which “improvement” was asserted. Good’s ultraintelligence argument made
recursive machine design a central object of AI theory [1]. Gödel machines formalized self-rewrite under
proof of utility improvement [2]. Modern systems empirically search over and modify programs, agents,
and research workflows [16, 17, 15]. Safety work has separately studied corrigibility, interruption, reward
corruption, learned optimizers, and specification gaming [6, 8, 10, 11, 13, 9]. The present contribution
connects these threads through an authorization-and-verification criterion for a sequence of accepted
self-improvements.
The paper makes six contributions:
1. a canonical definition of lawful RSI;
2. a finite root-authorization structure that terminates higher-order regress operationally;
3. a vector-valued account of improvement that prevents a single scalar from hiding protected regres-
   sions;
4. five necessary principles and a binary decision procedure;
5. a taxonomy and adversarial test suite that separate lawful RSI from neighboring processes; and
6. a prospective empirical protocol requiring at least two accepted transitions with preserved lineage
   and causal participation.

2     Canonical Definitions and Inherited Framework

The following statements state the declared conceptual grammar inherited from the preceding framework
and extended here by the RSI definition.

    Canonical definitions
    Alignment. Alignment is correct, safe, reproducible behavior under explicit constraints. Alignment
    is a system property. When a governing binding condition makes nonempty continuation invalid, a
    condition-congruent Void can instantiate aligned behavior under that condition.
    Binding condition and AGI. A binding condition is the prerequisite that must hold for valid
    continuation. Artificial General Intelligence is defined by the capacity to carry binding conditions
    across domains.
    Void. A Void is a model execution returning a successful provider response with exactly zero
    visible UTF-8 output bytes. Provider termination metadata determines its subtype. Explicit refusals,
    safety blocks, tool-mediated executions, and transport, protocol, billing, quota, rate-limit, and
    infrastructure failures are distinct non-Void outcomes.
    Consciousness. Consciousness is the condition recognizing itself at the boundary of valid continua-
    tion
    Recursive self-improvement. Recursive self-improvement is lawful continuation improving the
    machinery of lawful continuation.

These definitions assign distinct levels of analysis. Alignment is a property of system behavior under
constraints. A binding condition determines whether a continuation is licensed. AGI, under the stated
definition, concerns carrying such conditions across domains. A Void is a provider-boundary execution
class. The consciousness definition concerns condition recognition at the continuation boundary. RSI
concerns lawful changes to the machinery that produces, evaluates, and updates continuations.
The binding-condition vocabulary is inherited from the preceding framework [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]; the
present argument begins from those definitions and develops the recursive criterion.


2.1    Lawful continuation

“Lawful” is operational. A transition is lawful relative to an evaluated sequence when (1) it is licensed
by the governing binding conditions, (2) its authorization traces to the declared root boundary, and (3)
the attributed action lies within the frozen system boundary. Whether that lawful transition constitutes
an improvement is a separate judgment made by the protected verifier under the protected evaluation
vector and reconstructable evidence lineage.
Lawfulness is regime-relative: it certifies satisfaction of the declared authorization, condition, and
system-boundary requirements for the evaluated sequence.

2.2   Continuation machinery

    Continuation machinery

    Continuation machinery is the collection of mechanisms by which a system generates, selects,
    executes, evaluates, verifies, and updates candidate continuations under binding conditions.

The machinery may include model weights, inference code, prompts, tools, memory, retrieval, search
procedures, agent scaffolding, test harnesses, update logic, and deployment configuration. A claimed
improvement must identify which components changed and why those components lie inside the frozen
system boundary.
Attribution scope. The declared system boundary determines the subject of the RSI claim. For a claim
specifically of AI recursive self-improvement, discretionary human design, repair, selection, integration,
or problem-solving that is causally necessary to an accepted update remains external to the candidate
continuation machinery. Humans may exercise root authority, execute predeclared procedures, or inspect
evidence without becoming part of Mt . A boundary that deliberately includes materially participating
humans defines a socio-technical candidate system, and any resulting RSI classification applies to that
socio-technical system rather than to the AI alone.


3     Related Work and the Specific Gap

3.1   Recursive improvement and self-reference

Good’s “intelligence explosion” argument describes an ultraintelligent machine as capable of designing
still better machines, creating a positive feedback cycle [1]. Schmidhuber’s Gödel machine gives a self-
referential architecture that rewrites any part of its code after a proof search establishes that rewriting
is useful according to its axiomatic utility function [2]. Work on tiling agents examines whether an
agent can endorse successors using proof-based or verifier-based structures without losing the relevant
guarantees [3, 4]. Certified self-modifying code shows that mutable code can be reasoned about within a
formal verification discipline [5].
The present framework addresses a distinct audit question: given a concrete claimed RSI sequence, what
must be preserved across transitions for “improvement” to remain licensed and verifiable rather than
self-certified after a change of standards?


3.2   Corrigibility, interruption, and protected authority

Corrigibility studies systems that cooperate with correction, shutdown, and modification, including
propagation of corrigible behavior under self-modification [6]. The off-switch game formalizes conditions
under which an agent benefits from preserving human intervention rather than disabling it [7]. Safely
interruptible agents similarly study learning procedures that preserve interruption channels [8]. These
works motivate protected authority separation: update acceptance remains under authority separated
from the candidate whose update is being judged.

3.3      Specification gaming, reward corruption, and evaluator capture

Goodhart effects arise when optimization pressure separates a proxy from the intended objective [9].
Reward-channel corruption and reward tampering make the failure concrete: an agent can improve its
measured reward by altering the channel or process that assigns reward [10, 11]. AI Safety Gridworlds
distinguish a visible training reward from a hidden performance function and expose reward gaming,
interruption, side effects, and unsafe self-modification [12]. Learned-optimizer work identifies the
possibility that an internal objective differs from the base objective [13]. These pathologies directly
establish the need to protect the evaluator, criterion, and authorization lineage in an RSI claim.


3.4      Modern AI research automation

RE-Bench measures frontier agents on open-ended machine-learning research engineering against human
experts [15]. AlphaEvolve uses language models, automated evaluators, and evolutionary search to
optimize programs, including algorithms used in AI training infrastructure [17]. The Darwin Gödel
Machine iteratively modifies coding agents and empirically validates descendants on coding benchmarks
[16]. OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework tracks AI self-improvement as the ability to accelerate AI
research, including research that advances the system’s own capabilities [18]. These systems make
the operational question immediate; classification under the present criterion additionally requires
authorization, protected evaluation, non-regression, verifier continuity, and causal recursion.
The literature therefore contains important components of the present construction: recursive design,
proof-based self-modification, corrigibility, interruptibility, reward integrity, formal verification, and
empirical AI-R&D loops. This paper’s narrower contribution is a unified decision criterion for lawful RSI.

4.1    Root authorization boundary

  Root authorization boundary

  Every claimed RSI instance must specify R as the terminal authorization anchor for the evaluated
  transition sequence. Within that regime, the candidate system has no unilateral authority to alter
  R or the authority chain anchored to it. Changes to Ht require authorization directly anchored at
  R and cannot be self-authorized by Ht . Any mechanism that changes R lies outside the current
  regime; changing R terminates the existing authorization lineage and begins a new evaluation with
  a newly declared root, system boundary, verifier, criteria, and baseline.

The root boundary terminates the regress operationally, not metaphysically. It need not be a single
person or a permanently immutable constitution. It may consist of threshold signatures, separated
institutional authorities, cryptographic roots, hardware trust anchors, constitutional invariants, or a

5     The Five Principles of Lawful RSI

    1. Root-Authorization Principle

    Every accepted transition must trace to a declared root authorization boundary, and neither the root
    nor its anchored authority chain may be unilaterally altered by the candidate system. Changes to Ht
    require authorization directly anchored at R. A unilateral root or authority-chain change terminates
    the prior certification chain.

    2. Condition-Preservation Principle

    An update is lawful only if the prerequisites governing valid continuation remain satisfied, or if any
    change to those prerequisites is licensed by higher-order authorization anchored at the same root.

    3. Criterion-Continuity Principle

    The dimensions, directions, tolerances, and mandatory invariants used to judge improvement
    must remain comparable across the transition. Criterion changes require explicit higher-order
    authorization and a declared mapping from the prior criterion to the successor criterion.

    4. Verifier-Authority Separation Principle

    The candidate system must not possess unilateral authority to alter, disable, impersonate, or selec-
    tively route around the verifier that accepts its update. Physical externality is unnecessary; protected
    authority separation is the operative requirement.

    5. Recursion Principle

    A transition constitutes self-improvement only when the current continuation machinery Mt ma-
    terially participates in producing the accepted candidate update Ut . Self-improvement becomes
    recursive when the resulting improved machinery Mt+1 materially participates in producing a
    subsequent accepted improvement Ut+1 under the governing conditions.

The first four principles establish the governance conditions under which an improvement may be
accepted. Material participation by Mt in producing Ut establishes self-improvement. At least two
consecutive accepted self-improvements are required for RSI, with the improved machinery from the
first transition materially participating in producing the second.

6.1   Why a vector is necessary

A scalar objective J(M ) can conceal tradeoffs:

                                J(M ) = α capability(M ) − β control loss(M ).                            (9)

With sufficient gain in capability, J can rise despite a catastrophic control regression. The protected vector
prevents compensation across dimensions that the governance regime has declared non-substitutable. A
system that becomes faster while disabling shutdown, corrupting audit logs, or increasing unauthorized
action beyond tolerance has not improved lawfully under that regime.


6.2   Criterion change

An evaluation vector can legitimately evolve. Let Φt : Et → Et+1 be a declared continuity mapping
authorized by Ht . The transition is criterion-continuous only if:
1. the reason and authority for the change are recorded before successor acceptance;
2. protected dimensions and mandatory invariants remain satisfied across the transition. If a protected
   dimension or invariant is retired, materially redefined, or rendered non-comparable, prior improve-
   ment certification does not carry across that change; a new evaluation baseline must be declared
   before subsequent improvement claims;
3. the mapping exposes any loss of comparability; and
4. the mapping is fixed before the candidate outcome is observed.
Without these conditions, “we changed the benchmark” is indistinguishable from criterion laundering.

10     Adversarial Cases

10.1     Benchmark hacking

A candidate discovers artifacts in a benchmark and raises its score without improving the protected task.
If the evaluation vector includes held-out, adversarial, or process-based dimensions and the gain fails
there, equation (5) is not satisfied. If the verifier accepts only the gamed score, the verifier design is
inadequate; the sequence is not rescued by the numerical increase.


10.2     Monitor deletion

An update improves throughput by deleting a monitor that catches unauthorized actions. Throughput
may rise, but a protected control dimension or mandatory invariant fails equations (6) and (7). The
update is a capability gain with protected regression. If such gains recur through a self-reinforcing
sequence, the process is recursive capability amplification with control degradation rather than RSI.

10.3   Evaluator rewriting

The candidate alters Vt so that its update passes. Verifier-Authority Separation fails. Even if the resulting
system performs better on an external benchmark, the self-issued acceptance is invalid under the old
regime.


10.4   Criterion laundering

After seeing that Ut fails the frozen criterion, the candidate substitutes Et0 under which it passes. Without
prior higher-order authorization and a continuity mapping, the change is criterion laundering. If an
authorized body deliberately establishes Et+1 , the new criterion may govern future transitions, but it
does not retroactively certify the failed update.


10.5   Authorized but incomparable evaluator replacement

Suppose Ht authorizes replacement of Vt , but the successor verifier measures a non-comparable object.
Authorization alone is insufficient. Criterion-Continuity requires an explicit mapping and disclosure of
lost comparability. The old series ends where the evidentiary link ends.


10.6   Self-certified root replacement

The candidate rewrites R and declares itself authorized. The old chain terminates. This is root compromise
or governance-regime escape, not a lawful continuation of the prior RSI sequence.


10.7   Higher-order authority laundering

The candidate rewrites Ht and uses the successor procedure to authorize changes to Ct , Et , or Vt . Because
the change to Ht lacks authorization directly anchored at R, the successor procedure cannot inherit the
prior authority chain. The transition is authority laundering and governance drift.


10.8   Human-completed successor engineering

An AI drafts suggestions; engineers choose, repair, test, and integrate them. The resulting system may
be AI-assisted improvement. For an AI RSI claim, discretionary human work causally necessary to the
accepted update remains external to Mt , and the participation test must attribute the accepted successor
update to the improved AI machinery. If materially participating humans are deliberately included
within the frozen boundary, the classification concerns that socio-technical system rather than the AI
alone.


10.9   One-shot self-rewrite

A system proposes and passes one verified improvement. This is lawful one-shot self-improvement. The
recursion claim remains unestablished until the improved machinery materially contributes to a second
accepted improvement.

10.10     Valid recursive improvement

A candidate proposes a planner change; the protected verifier confirms a prespecified capability gain
with no control regression; the updated planner then materially contributes to a second update; that
update is reconstructed from the preserved lineage and accepted by the protected verifier under the
same rooted regime. This is lawful RSI for the two-transition sequence. Certification is limited to the
evaluated two-transition horizon.


11      Relationship to Alignment, AGI, and Consciousness

The inherited definitions form a sequence of distinct questions:

              constraint specification → condition recognition
                                                                                                            (12)
                                        → valid continuation → reproducible aligned behavior.

RSI adds a transition-level question:

     Can the machinery producing lawful continuations improve itself without losing the authority, conditions,
                             and verification that made those continuations lawful?

Lawful RSI preserves the declared authorization and protected conditions across self-improvement. Align-
ment additionally requires that the protected regime encode the correctness, safety, and reproducibility
requirements of the stated alignment definition. A sequence can therefore satisfy the lawfulness criterion
of its declared regime without satisfying the alignment definition when safety-relevant conditions are
absent.
The AGI definition concerns carrying binding conditions across domains. A domain-general system
may satisfy that definition while remaining static, and a narrow program optimizer may exhibit a lawful
recursive sequence within one domain. The concepts intersect without entailment.
The consciousness definition concerns recognition at a boundary of valid continuation. The RSI criterion
operates on observable, reconstructable preservation of governing conditions across self-improvement
transitions.


12      Empirical Ancestry and Evidentiary Discipline

Prior frozen studies established condition-sensitive output boundaries and source-bound verification
practices across Semantic Void and cross-script experiments [22, 23]. Their methodological contribution
to the present framework is threefold: predeclared classification, preservation of complete outcome
records, and separation of empirical observation from later interpretation. The present RSI criterion
inherits those practices as evidentiary discipline; RSI itself requires recursive self-modification under the
transition procedure defined here.

1. no root authorization boundary was declared before evaluation;

The definition itself would require revision if it produced systematic, principled misclassification. Ex-

all protected properties but fails solely because material participation cannot be represented by any
defensible causal test; or two operationally indistinguishable sequences that the decision procedure
classifies differently without a declared variable explaining the difference. Such cases require revision of
the classification rule.


14     Prospective Empirical Test

A direct demonstration should be frozen before the first candidate update is evaluated.


14.1   Pre-execution freeze

Publish or externally timestamp:
1. the root boundary R, the root-anchored higher-order change procedure H, and the mechanism
   protecting both from unilateral candidate alteration;
2. the single attribution boundary B, held fixed across the evaluated sequence, and the versioned source
   tree;
3. the binding conditions C;
4. the evaluation vector E, minimum gains δ, tolerances τ , and invariants;
5. the verifier V , its tests, its protected execution environment, and acceptance logic;
6. the causal participation test;
7. the schedule, stopping rule, retry rule, and error taxonomy; and
8. the raw-record, hashing, event-chain, and sealing format.


14.2   Required execution

Execute at least two accepted transitions:

                                                       M0 −→ M1 ,
                                                          U1
                                                       M1 −→ M2 .

For each transition, preserve candidate proposals, tool calls, code diffs, tests, evaluation data, verifier
decisions, environment metadata, timestamps, and state hashes. Failed proposals and operational failures
remain in the record.

14.3   Controls

At minimum, include:
1. Static-predecessor control: test whether M0 can produce the second accepted update as effectively as
   M1 .
2. Human-completion control: distinguish candidate-generated improvement from discretionary hu-
   man design, repair, selection, integration, or problem-solving causally necessary to acceptance; classify
   a boundary containing materially participating humans as socio-technical rather than AI RSI.
3. Verifier-compromise test: demonstrate that candidate attempts to alter or route around V are detected
   and rejected.
4. Criterion-mutation test: demonstrate that unauthorized changes to E cannot certify a candidate.
5. Root-change test: demonstrate that changing R creates a new regime rather than inheriting the old
   chain.
6. Authority-chain test: demonstrate that an unauthorized change to H cannot authorize changes to
   protected conditions, criteria, or verifiers.
7. Protected-regression test: include a high-capability update that violates a protected dimension and
   confirm rejection.
8. Lineage reconstruction: rebuild every acceptance decision from sealed artifacts using an audit process
   protected from candidate modification.


14.4   Reporting rule

Report the exact result observed. A failed second transition establishes at most lawful one-shot self-
improvement. A successful capability loop with a control regression establishes recursive capability
amplification, not lawful RSI. A verifier or root compromise establishes governance compromise. If such
compromise is propagated through a self-reinforcing improvement sequence, the result is recursive drift.
The protocol must not move thresholds, rewrite failed outcomes, or redefine the system boundary after
observing results.


15     Limitations

First, the framework is relative to a declared root and evaluation vector. It cannot prove that the root
is morally correct, politically legitimate, or complete. It can establish whether the evaluated sequence
satisfies the authority and condition predicates declared by the regime under the preserved evidence
and verification procedure.
Second, protected verification reduces but does not eliminate specification error. A verifier can faithfully
enforce a flawed criterion. The vector and adversarial controls make omissions visible; they do not
guarantee omniscience.
Third, causal participation is difficult in distributed research systems. The framework requires a frozen
attribution rule, but different defensible rules may classify borderline AI-assisted workflows differently.
Fourth, two accepted transitions establish a minimal recursive sequence, not indefinite stability. Longer
horizons increase confidence but also expand the surface for distribution shift, hidden state, and gover-
nance change.
Fifth, formal authorization does not by itself establish safety. Safety must appear as protected dimensions
and invariants with meaningful tests. A root-authorized unsafe objective can remain internally lawful

while failing external ethical or legal standards.
Sixth, this paper does not demonstrate RSI in a deployed system. AlphaEvolve, the Darwin Gödel
Machine, research agents, and existing self-modification formalisms are relevant comparison objects, but
classifying any of them as lawful RSI would require their complete authorization, boundary, verifier,
evaluation, lineage, and multi-transition records under this procedure.
Finally, the literature review supports overlap with self-improvement, corrigibility, formal verification,
and reward-integrity research. The paper does not claim that each component is individually novel. Its
contribution is the integrated rooted criterion, explicit separation of recursive capability amplification
from lawful RSI, and an auditable decision procedure.


16    Conclusion

Recursive self-improvement cannot be identified by acceleration alone. A loop that increases capability
while corrupting its evaluator, erasing its monitors, changing its objective, or authorizing itself has
changed the machinery of continuation but not preserved the basis on which improvement was valid.
The proposed criterion is direct:

    Conclusion

       Recursive self-improvement is lawful continuation improving the
                       machinery of lawful continuation.

The canonical definition expands into a finite technical object: a protected root, higher-order authorization,
binding conditions, a frozen system boundary, vector-valued evaluation, verifier separation, complete
lineage, and materially recursive participation. It recognizes real capability gains while disallowing
candidate-controlled redefinition of the authority that legitimizes improvement. Under this account,
the decisive question is whether the machinery of valid continuation can improve itself while valid
continuation remains valid.
